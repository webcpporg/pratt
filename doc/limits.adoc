[#pratt-limits]
== Limits

Pratt parsing recurses: each operand that a nud or a led asks for is a call
of `expression`, below the calls of the nud and the led that asked, so every
level of nesting in the input costs stack. A line of ten thousand `(` would
nest ten thousand calls, and overflow the stack: a crash, not an error.

[#pratt-limits-depth]
=== The depth limit

The parser counts its active `expression` calls, and refuses the call that
would make them more than its limit, `max_depth`, with `nesting_too_deep`,
before it reads a token. So the stack never grows past what the limit
allows, and the input that would have overflowed it is an error like any
other. `max_depth` is an argument of the parser's constructor, of `parse`
and of `calc::evaluate`, and its default is `pratt::default_max_depth`,
which is 64. A negative `max_depth` behaves as 0, which refuses every parse.

A `1` inside a number of parentheses is that number plus one calls deep:

[source]
----
include::{examples}/pratt_depth.cpp[tag=depth,indent=0]
----

It prints:

[listing]
----
include::{examples}/pratt_depth.expected[]
----

The limit bounds the recursion through `expression` alone. That is the
whole recursion of a grammar whose nuds and leds parse every operand through
`expression` or the helpers, as the calculator's and the micro-subset's do,
the micro-subset's argument loop included. A nud or a led that recurses
through functions of its own, without calling `expression`, is not bounded
by it, and bounds that recursion itself.

[#pratt-limits-stack]
=== The stack each level costs

The default is measured, not chosen. The measurement is the deepest nesting
that the calculator evaluated without a trap, over `calc::environment<>`,
with wasi-sdk 34 (clang 23.1.0) and wasmtime 47.0.3, and the stack divided
by that depth, for three shapes of N operators or `(`. It was taken on
wasm's default stack of 64{nbsp}KiB, in a debug build (`-O0 -fno-inline`) on
wasip2 and wasip3, and in a build with `-O2` on wasip2; natively in a debug
build, on a stack of 8{nbsp}MiB:

[cols="2,2,2,2",options="header"]
|===
| Shape, N operators or `(` | wasip2, wasip3 `-O0` | wasip2 `-O2` | native `-O0`, 8{nbsp}MiB

| `+((...(1)...))+`
| 232 (282 B)
| 450 (146 B)
| 16854 (497 B)

| `+- - ... - 1+`
| 263 (249 B)
| 450 (146 B)
| 18016 (465 B)

| `+2 ^ 2 ^ ... ^ 2+`
| 240 (273 B)
| 405 (162 B)
| 12741 (657 B)
|===

Of these three, the shape that costs the most is a grouping, by a few bytes
a level. Over every shape of the calculator's grammar and of
<<pratt-openui,openui-lang's>>, the deepest debug parse that wasm's default
stack holds is at least 203 calls: openui-lang's call shape, on wasip2. The
calculator's least is 214, a grouping with a `pow` inside. The default is
the largest power of two at most half of that: half of 203 is 101, hence
64. A parse at the default depth uses at most half the stack, in the worst
shape and the slowest build, on every target pratt is tested on; a build
with `-O2` and a native stack hold far more.

In a debug build on wasm, a level of grouping costs 272 bytes, a level of
`+^+` 256 and a level of sign 240, plus at most about 7.5{nbsp}KiB that a parse
needs at any depth, its heaviest operand, a `pow`, included.

[#pratt-limits-raising]
=== Raising the limit

A program that raises the limit raises its stack with it, by the cost of a
level times the levels it adds. On wasm, the stack is set when the program
is linked, with the linker's flag `-Wl,-z,stack-size=` and the size in
bytes. A program that evaluates groupings 1024 deep in a debug build needs
about 1024 times 272 bytes and 7.5{nbsp}KiB, about 280{nbsp}KiB, so it links
with a stack of 1{nbsp}MiB, which keeps the margin the default keeps:

[source,bash]
----
clang++ --target=wasm32-wasip2 -std=c++20 \
    -Wl,-z,stack-size=1048576 \
    program.cpp -o program.wasm
----

and passes `max_depth` to every call:
`calc::evaluate(line, env, 1024)`. A b2 project adds the flag to its
target's requirements:

[listing]
----
<linkflags>-Wl,-z,stack-size=1048576
----

Natively, the stack is the thread's. The main thread's is the system's,
commonly 8{nbsp}MiB on Linux and macOS (`ulimit -s` shows it), and a thread a
program creates takes the size it is created with, through
`pthread_attr_setstacksize` or Boost.Thread's
`boost::thread::attributes::set_stack_size`. The measurement above holds
12741 levels of `+^+` on 8{nbsp}MiB in a debug build, so a native program has
room for a limit far above the default without changing its stack.
