[#pratt-calculator]
== The calculator

`<webcpp/pratt/calculator.hpp>` is a grammar on the engine, ready to use:
numbers and variables, the arithmetic and comparison operators of C, and
assignment, increment and decrement. `calc::evaluate(source, env)` parses
one line and computes its value as it parses, reading and writing its
variables in an environment, `env`, which keeps them from one call to the
next. Every operator goes through the engine's helpers; the calculator adds
no parsing of its own.

A program needs the header, and Boost.Decimal's, whose default number type
the calculator computes in:

[source]
----
include::{examples}/pratt_calculator.cpp[tag=includes,indent=0]
----

`evaluate` returns a `pratt::result` of the environment's number type, here
`boost::decimal::decimal64_t`. This program writes each line, then its value
in Boost.Decimal's shortest form, or its error:

[source]
----
include::{examples}/pratt_calculator.cpp[tag=print,indent=0]
----

It evaluates a few lines in one environment, `calc::environment<>`:

[source]
----
include::{examples}/pratt_calculator.cpp[tag=values,indent=0]
----

It prints:

[listing]
----
include::{examples}/pratt_calculator.expected[lines=1..8]
----

[#pratt-calculator-language]
=== The language

A number is digits, then optionally a point and more digits: `42`, `0.5`. A
name is a letter or `+_+`, then letters, digits and `+_+`. Space, tab, carriage
return and line feed separate tokens. The operators, from the loosest to
the tightest, with the binding powers the grammar gives them:

[cols="2,1,1,4",options="header"]
|===
| Operators | Power | Groups | What it gives

| `=`
| 10
| right
| Writes the value on its right into the variable on its left, and gives
  that value; `c = d = 5` sets both.

| `+== != < <= > >=+`
| 20
| left
| 1 when the comparison holds, 0 when it does not.

| `{plus}` `-`
| 30
| left
| The sum and the difference.

| `+*+` `/` `%`
| 40
| left
| The product, the quotient and the remainder, whose sign is the left
  operand's.

| `{plus}` `-` `{plus}{plus}` `--` before an operand
| 45
| -
| The value, its negation; the variable plus or minus one, written back,
  as its new value.

| `+^+`
| 50
| right
| The left operand to the power of the right one.

| `{plus}{plus}` `--` after an operand
| 70
| -
| The variable plus or minus one, written back, as its old value.
|===

A sign binds less tightly than `+^+`, so `-2 ^ 2` is -4, and `+^+` groups
to the right, so `2 ^ 3 ^ 2` is 512, as the output above shows.

A variable keeps its value in the environment, from one line to the next:

[source]
----

----

It prints:

[listing]
----

----

`{plus}{plus}` and `--` behave as in C, and only on a variable: before it,
they give the new value, and after it, the old one. A node of the calculator
holds a value and, for a bare variable, its name, which is what `=`,
`{plus}{plus}` and `--` write. A grouping keeps the name of what it holds,
so `(a) = 1` writes `a` and `(a){plus}{plus}` increments it, while
`(1) = 2` has no name to
write, and is an error. A variable that no line has written is an error
only where it is read: `a = 5` writes a name the environment does not hold
yet.

[#pratt-calculator-exactness]
=== Exactness and the number type

The calculator computes in `boost::decimal::decimal64_t` by default, a
decimal floating-point type of 16 significant digits, for exactness: a
decimal fraction written in the input, `0.1` or `19.99`, is exactly that
number, so `0.1 + 0.2 == 0.3` holds and `price * quantity` is `59.97`, not a
binary approximation of it. The lexer builds each number in the number
type's own arithmetic, its digits as one integer divided once by ten to the
number of its fraction digits, and never through a `double`.

A literal longer than the type's precision keeps its first 17 significant
digits in `decimal64_t` and in `double` (`digits10 + 1`, or `max_digits10`
where that is more): a later digit of the integer part multiplies the value
by ten, and a later digit of the fraction is dropped. So `1.` followed by
any number of zeros is exactly 1, a literal up to the type's largest value
is finite, the largest `double` written out in full included, and `1`
followed by 400 zeros is an infinity. A long literal is within one unit
in the last place of the nearest `decimal64_t`, and within a few of the
nearest `double`, whose digits round as they are read. A fraction keeps its value down to the
type's smallest subnormal, and is 0 below it.

An integral power is computed by repeated squaring, in the number type's
own multiplications, for exactness and determinism: every product is exact
while it fits the type's precision, so `43 + 12 + 2 * (44 - 12) ^ 4` is
exactly 2097207, and the result is the same bits on every platform, where
a library's `pow` may round differently from one to the next. Past the
precision, each product rounds, and the relative error grows with the
exponent, where a correctly rounded `pow` would not: in `double`,
`1.000000001 ^ 1000000000` differs from the result of `std::pow` in its
ninth significant digit. That is the price of the same bits on every
platform.

The same bits assume each compiler's default for fusing a multiplication
and an addition, which every operation of the calculator keeps apart in
statements of their own. Built with `-ffp-contract=fast` or
`-ffast-math`, a compiler may fuse them anyway, and a `double` result may
then differ in its last place from one platform to another.

The power's sign is the base's when the exponent is odd. A negative
integral exponent is one divided by the positive power, so `2 ^ -3` is
`0.125`, and `0 ^ 0` is 1. When the positive power overflows, or underflows
to 0, `pow` computes the result instead, so `2 ^ -1074` is the smallest
positive `double` and `0.1 ^ -400` is an infinity. Otherwise only an
exponent that is not an integer calls `pow`.

Division by zero is an error, `division_by_zero`, for `/` and for `%`, and
for `0 ^ -1`, which divides by zero too. A floating-point type would answer
an infinity or a NaN, and the line after would compute on with it; the
calculator stops at the line that asked.

Any type that models `calc::number` serves. It is copyable and constructible
from `int`, has the binary `+ - / *`, the unary `-` and the six
comparisons, and `pow`, `fmod` and `trunc`, found as `using std::pow;
pow(a, b)` finds them, each returning the type itself. Boost.Decimal's types
model it, and so do `float`, `double` and `long double`. The number type is
the environment's: `calc::environment<double>` computes in `double`.

[source]
----
include::{examples}/pratt_double.cpp[tag=double,indent=0]
----

It prints, in the shortest form of `std::to_chars`:

[listing]
----
include::{examples}/pratt_double.expected[]
----

The integral powers are still exact, `2 ^ 60` included, since repeated
squaring multiplies powers of two, which `double` holds exactly. The
decimal fractions are not: `0.1` and `0.2` are the nearest binary
fractions, whose sum is not the nearest one to `0.3`. `2.25 ^ 0.5` is not
an integral power, and is the result of `std::pow`. Its exact value, 1.5,
is a `double`, so any `pow` that errs by less than an ulp returns it
exactly, on every standard library.

[#pratt-calculator-environment]
=== The environment

The environment is where the variables live, and its type is the
program's choice. A type `E` models `calc::environment_for` when its
`number_type` models `calc::number` and it has two functions:

* `lookup(name)`, which returns a pointer to the value of `name`, or
  `nullptr` when the name is not set;
* `assign(name, value)`, which sets it and returns a `pratt::result<void>`,
  so that it can refuse, a storage that is full for instance, with an error
  of its own.

The name is a `std::string_view` into the line, so an environment that
keeps a name copies it. `calc::environment<Number, Allocator>` is the
calculator's own, a `std::map` whose nodes and keys allocate through the
program's allocator (<<pratt-allocation>>). This one is a program's own,
two variables in fixed storage, in `double`:

----

[#pratt-calculator-errors]
=== Errors, in reading order

Every error of the calculator is a value of `pratt::result`, one of pratt's
errors (<<pratt-errors>>) or the environment's own:

[source]
----
include::{examples}/pratt_calculator.cpp[tag=errors,indent=0]
----

It prints:

[listing]
----
include::{examples}/pratt_calculator.expected[lines=17..25]
----

The lexer is lazy: it reads one token ahead, and the parse reads the tokens
in order, so the first error in reading order is the one returned. A
token's error is found when the token is read, and an operator's own error,
an undefined operand or a zero divisor, once the operator has its operands.
A character that is no token, such as `$`, is a token of its own, of the
highest binding power, whose nud and led are `invalid_character`, so the
engine reaches it wherever it stands, even after a whole expression, and
`1 $ 2` is `invalid_character` rather than `trailing_input`.

A write made before the error stays made: nothing is undone.

[source]
----
include::{examples}/pratt_calculator.cpp[tag=writes,indent=0]
----

It prints:

[listing]
----
include::{examples}/pratt_calculator.expected[lines=26..29]
----

`i{plus}{plus} $` increments `i` before it reads the `$`, so the line fails
and `i` is 2. `x + 1 $` is `invalid_character` although `x` is undefined:
`{plus}` finds that its left operand has no value only once it has its
right one, and reading the right one reads the `$` first. `5 = 3 $` is
`invalid_assignment_target`, since `=` checks its target before it reads
its right operand. A program that needs a line to write all or nothing
evaluates it in a copy of the environment, and keeps the copy when the line
succeeds.
