[#pratt-introduction]
== Introduction

A parser turns text into structure: `1 + 2 * 3` into an addition whose right
operand is a product. Pratt parsing, or top-down operator precedence, after
Vaughan Pratt's paper of 1973, does it with one loop and one question asked
of every token: what it means at the start of an expression, what it means
after one, and how tightly it holds what stands on its left. Precedence and
associativity become numbers, and a new operator is a new answer to that
question, not a new rule of a grammar.

pratt is that loop, and nothing else: an engine that knows no token, no
operator and no tree. A program gives it a token stream and a grammar, and
the engine calls the grammar for each token it reads. The grammar decides
what a parse builds, a value computed on the way, a tree, or a string, and
every failure comes back as a value. Beside the engine, pratt ships a
calculator built on it, `calc`, which computes exactly in decimal by default
(<<pratt-calculator>>).

[#pratt-introduction-nud-led]
=== nud, led and lbp

Pratt names the three answers of a token:

* *nud*, its null denotation: what it means with nothing on its left. A
  digit is a number, `-` is the negation of what follows it, `(` opens a
  grouping.
* *led*, its left denotation: what it means after a complete expression,
  which becomes its left operand. `-` is a subtraction there, and `+*+` a
  product.
* *lbp*, its left binding power: how tightly it binds that left operand. A
  token of power 0, such as the end of the input or a closing parenthesis,
  takes no left operand, and ends an expression.

The engine's whole algorithm is one function, `expression(rbp)`. It reads a
token and calls its nud; then, as long as the next token's lbp is greater
than `rbp`, it reads that token and calls its led with what it has parsed so
far. A nud or a led that needs an operand calls `expression` again, with the
binding power of its own operator, so the recursion stops at the first
operator that binds less tightly. A `+*+` that asks for its right operand with
its power, 20, stops before a `{plus}` of power 10, which then takes the product
as its left operand: precedence is that comparison of two numbers.

One token can have both denotations, as `-` has, and its nud and its led
are two functions that never meet. That is what makes Pratt parsing fit the
languages people write, where the same character is a sign, an operator, a
grouping or a call, depending on where it stands.

=== A first grammar

This program parses integers of one digit with a unary minus and the
binary `{plus}`, `-` and `+*+`, and computes their values while it parses.
It begins with the engine's header and an alias for its namespace:

[source]
----
include::{examples}/pratt_first_grammar.cpp[tag=includes,indent=0]
----

The engine reads tokens from a token stream: here, the characters of a text,
each one a token, with `'\0'` once the text is spent. `peek` shows the next
token, `advance` consumes it, and `at_end` says whether anything is left:

[source]
----
include::{examples}/pratt_first_grammar.cpp[tag=stream,indent=0]
----

The grammar answers the three questions. Its `node_type`, what a parse
builds, is `int`, since this grammar computes as it goes. `lbp` gives
`{plus}` and `-` the power 10 and `+*+` the power 20. The nud of a digit
is its value, and the nud of `-` asks the parser for an operand with the
power 30, above every binary operator, so that the minus takes only the
next number. The led of a binary operator asks for the right operand with
the operator's own power, then combines the two:

[source]
----
include::{examples}/pratt_first_grammar.cpp[tag=grammar,indent=0]
----

`pratt::parse` parses a whole input with a grammar, and returns a
`pratt::result<int>`, the value or the error:

[source]
----
include::{examples}/pratt_first_grammar.cpp[tag=main,indent=0]
----

It prints:

[listing]
----
include::{examples}/pratt_first_grammar.expected[]
----

`1+2*3` and `2*3+4` both multiply first, whatever their order, since
`+*+` binds more tightly than `{plus}`. `8-2-1` is 5, `(8-2)-1`, because the
led of the first `-` asks for its right operand with the power 10, and the
second `-`, of the same power, is not greater, so it ends that operand and
takes `8-2` as its own left one: an operator that asks with its own power
is left-associative. `-2*3` negates only the 2, as the minus asks with 30.
The last three fail, each with an error of pratt's: the input ends where an
operand was due, a `+*+` stands where no nud has a meaning for it, and the
`2` of `12`, a second number with no operator before it, is input left
over after a whole expression.

[#pratt-requirements]
=== Requirements

* *A {cpp}20 compiler.* pratt's tests and examples are built and run on
  every native lane of webcpp's continuous integration, on emscripten under
  node, and for wasm32-wasip2 and wasm32-wasip3 with the Clang of wasi-sdk,
  under wasmtime (<<pratt-testing>>).
* *Boost's headers, 1.92 or newer.* The engine uses Boost.System, for
  `result` and the error codes, and the calculator Boost.Decimal, for its
  default number type. pratt is header-only, and nothing of Boost is
  compiled for it.
* *webcpp's configuration,*
  link:{webcpp-libs}/config/{webcpp-page}#config-exceptions[config], whose
  `<webcpp/config.hpp>` every header of pratt includes first. It is
  header-only too.
* *Exceptions are the program's choice.* pratt throws nothing of its own,
  and no header of it throws, tries or catches, so the same headers serve a
  build with exceptions and one without. webcpp builds pratt with exceptions
  on every target, and its lint compiles every public header, and every test
  and example, without them; whether a program uses them is up to whoever
  builds it. A program without exceptions defines `boost::throw_exception`,
  which Boost.System's `result` names, as
  link:{webcpp-libs}/xactor/{webcpp-page}#xactor-quick-start-build[xactor's page]
  shows.
* *RTTI is never restricted.* Every target is built with it, and pratt asks
  no program to turn it off.
* *Nothing else:* no threads, no clock, no global or static mutable state,
  and no allocation in the engine (<<pratt-allocation>>).

[#pratt-reading]
=== Reading this page

`<webcpp/pratt.hpp>` includes the engine, `<webcpp/pratt/errors.hpp>` and
`<webcpp/pratt/parser.hpp>`, whose names are in the namespace
`webcpp::pratt`. The calculator, `<webcpp/pratt/calculator.hpp>`, is
included by its own name, so that a program of the engine alone does not
read Boost.Decimal; its names are in `webcpp::pratt::calc`. The code of this
page writes them `pratt::parse` and `calc::evaluate`, as after
`namespace pratt = webcpp::pratt;` and `namespace calc = webcpp::pratt::calc;`.

<<pratt-grammar>> takes the parts a program writes: the token stream, the
grammar, the node, and the helpers that write the usual kinds of operator.
<<pratt-calculator>> shows the calculator, its number types and its
environments, and <<pratt-openui>> a grammar with calls, the seed of a
parser of openui-lang, with the two programs that read lines and answer
them. <<pratt-errors>> lists every error, <<pratt-limits>> the depth limit
and the stack it protects, and <<pratt-allocation>> what allocates and
through which allocator. <<pratt-testing>> shows how a program tests its
grammar, and how pratt itself is tested. The <<reference,reference>> closes
the page.
